However, integrating exteroception remains a significant challenge: existing approaches often fail to achieve high-speed, stable locomotion across diverse, unstructured terrains. In this work, we present Hiking in the Wild , an extensible, end-to-end perceptive legged parkour framework designed for robust humanoid hiking. Our approach maps depth inputs and proprioception directly to joint actions, without relying on external localization and mapping. To ensure safety and training stability, we introduce two key mechanisms: a foothold safety mechanism combining extensible Terrain Edge Detection with Foot Volume Points to reduce catastrophic slippage on edges, and a Flat Patch Sampling strategy that mitigates reward hacking by generating feasible navigation targets. Extensive experiments on the Unitree G1 humanoid robot demonstrate that our policy enables robust traversal of complex terrains at speeds up to 2.5 m/s. Code and video demonstrations are available on the project page: https://project-instinct.github.io/hiking-in-the-wild . Figure 1: Hiking in the Wild. Our framework enables a humanoid robot to traverse diverse terrains in the wild. The robot can run at a maximum speed of 2.5 m/s over complex terrain. It can also negotiate stairs, gaps, high platforms, and ramps. Relying on depth images for perception, our extensible end-to-end framework achieves robust performance across these scenarios. Keywords: Humanoid Locomotion, Perceptive Legged Parkour, Reinforcement Learning 1 Introduction Humanoid robots hold immense promise for traversing complex real-world environments and executing difficult tasks. Unlike their wheeled counterparts, humanoids can step over obstacles and cross discontinuous terrain. Recently, significant strides have been made in humanoid control, enabling robots to perform dynamic maneuvers such as dancing, backflips, and mimicking human motions [ 24 , 26 ] . However, there is a fundamental distinction between tracking a predefined motion and hiking in the wild. While tracking resembles memorizing a routine, hiking requires the robot to actively perceive the terrain, adapt to irregularities, and handle the unknown. In this paper, we study legged parkour : agile traversal over obstacles using only legged contacts, without relying on arms, torso contacts, or full-body maneuvers such as vaulting and climbing. Blind locomotion has established a robust baseline [ 13 , 35 , 34 , 39 ] , but its purely reactive nature is fundamentally limited: without perceiving ahead, the robot cannot safely handle deep gaps or high steps. Integrating exteroceptive perception remains challenging: LiDAR-based methods [ 25 , 16 , 3 ] depend on precise state estimation and suffer from motion distortion at high speed, while depth-based methods that reconstruct heightmaps [ 7 , 41 , 40 ] are limited to low-speed, structured environments and struggle to scale to unseen wild terrains. To address these challenges, we present Hiking in the Wild , an extensible, end-to-end perceptive legged parkour framework designed for robust humanoid locomotion in unstructured environments. Unlike complex modular pipelines, our approach directly maps depth input and proprioception to joint actions. To handle the high dimensionality of visual data and the complexity of diverse terrain skills, we incorporate a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture [ 18 , 17 ] . Our design allows the policy to capture depth inputs at high frequencies (up to 60 Hz). This high-bandwidth perception loop enables the robot to perform dynamic legged obstacle traversal and high-speed running (up to 2.5 m/s). Crucially, our system incorporates a realistic depth synthesis module that models sensor noise and artifacts during training, enabling zero-shot sim-to-real transfer without external mapping and localization of the environment. A critical yet overlooked issue in humanoid RL is foothold precision: stepping partially on an edge can cause catastrophic slippage. Prior approaches [ 54 , 4 ] require manually designed constraints for specific terrain types, limiting extensibility. We instead propose a geometric Terrain Edge Detector coupled with foot-attached Volume Points that automatically detects edges on arbitrary meshes and penalizes foot penetration, implicitly teaching the policy to seek safe, flat surfaces. Training stability is further challenged by “reward hacking”, where random velocity commands lead the robot to spin rather than traverse obstacles [ 4 ] . We address this with a Flat Patch Sampling strategy that identifies reachable flat regions and derives speed-controlled commands toward them, ensuring the robot is always challenged with physically solvable tasks. We validate our framework on the Unitree G1 humanoid robot, demonstrating robust traversal of stairs, ramps, gaps, and platforms at speeds up to 2.5 m/s. The contributions of this work are: 1. An extensible, end-to-end perceptive framework for training and deployment based on depth input, enabling zero-shot sim-to-real transfer without external localization and mapping. 2. A novel safety mechanism using Terrain Edge Detection and Foot Volume Points, alleviating the precise foothold problem in learning-based control. 3. A geometry-aware command generation method that derives velocity commands by pointing toward reachable flat terrain patches, eliminating reward hacking and ensuring the robot actively traverses complex environments. 4. Demonstration of robust wild hiking and legged parkour capabilities on a humanoid robot. The training and deployment code is open-source and available to the community , enabling deployment on real robots with minimal hardware modifications. 2 Related Work 2.1 Learning-based Legged Locomotion Legged locomotion control has shifted from model-based approaches, such as Model Predictive Control (MPC) [ 10 , 38 , 21 ] , to data-driven reinforcement learning (RL) to better handle unmodeled dynamics. Early blind RL policies achieved impressive robustness on irregular terrains using only proprioception [ 35 , 39 , 13 , 49 ] . However, these methods lack the exteroception required to navigate large obstacles quickly and safely. To enable greater agility, recent works have integrated exteroceptive perception into the control loop [ 4 , 36 , 47 , 51 ] . One category of methods relies on LiDAR to build elevation maps (2.5D) [ 16 , 25 , 44 , 42 ] or voxel grids [ 3 ] . Nevertheless, these approaches depend heavily on precise localization, which limits their update frequency and overall robustness. Furthermore, LiDAR often suffers from motion distortion during fast movement, restricting these methods to low-speed scenarios. Alternatively, depth-based methods have been proposed. Some of these operate at low frequency [ 54 ] , while others employ intermediate modules to predict heightmaps [ 7 , 41 , 40 ] or learn end-to-end depth policies [ 43 , 14 ] , potentially compromising performance in high-speed or unseen wild environments. In contrast, our method directly utilizes high-frequency depth images as input to train an end-to-end policy, achieving highly dynamic behaviors across complex, unstructured terrains in the wild. 2.2 Perceptive Foothold Control Explicit foothold control typically decouples perception and planning, utilizing terrain representations like elevation maps to solve for optimal placements. Approaches range from heuristic search [ 46 , 8 , 20 , 22 , 6 , 31 , 1 ] and learned feasibility costs [ 28 , 48 , 29 , 45 ] to rigorous nonlinear optimization [ 12 , 30 ] . While hybrid frameworks that utilize probabilistic map uncertainty [ 20 , 9 ] or combine pre-planned references with online tracking [ 19 , 11 , 23 ] have achieved impressive agility, they remain brittle to state-estimation drift and reconstruction artifacts. Recently, end-to-end policies [ 4 ] use edge penalization to learn safe footholds implicitly. However, it is not extensible to arbitrary meshes. Our method takes a triangular mesh as input and automatically detects the edges, retaining the robustness of the implicit paradigm while improving safety. 3 Method Figure 2: System overview. The actor receives a history of h proprioceptive states (angular velocity, projected gravity, velocity commands, joint positions/velocities, last action) concatenated with a strided depth history ℋ t , with stochastic noise applied during training. During hardware deployment, velocity commands are provided by a human operator through a remote controller. An asymmetric critic additionally observes noise-free states and base linear velocity. The policy outputs target joint positions executed via PD control ( 𝝉 t = k p ​ ( 𝐚 t − 𝐪 t ) − k d ​ 𝐪 ˙ t ) with gains from [ 24 ] . Episodes terminate on time-out, boundary violation, torso contact, or large base tilt (details in subsection A.2 ). The reward comprises task tracking, regularization, safety, and AMP-style terms; full specifications are in Table 5 . 3.2 Ego-centric Depth Perception Figure 3: Depth processing: Top row shows ℱ s ​ i ​ m on synthetic data, and bottom row shows ℱ r ​ e ​ a ​ l on real-world data. We implement a GPU-accelerated ray-caster using NVIDIA Warp [ 27 ] to synthesize dense depth maps in real time. To bridge the sim-to-real gap, we define two alignment pipelines: ℱ s ​ i ​ m degrades simulated depth via sequential stochastic operations (range-dependent Gaussian noise, disparity artifact synthesis, blur, and OOD perturbation), while ℱ r ​ e ​ a ​ l applies inpainting and blur to the physical sensor stream, harmonizing both distributions into a shared perception space (see Figure 3 and subsection A.4 ). The policy receives a depth history of m frames with temporal stride ℓ . During deployment, depth acquisition and preprocessing run asynchronously at 60 Hz, while the policy runs at 50 Hz using the latest available depth observations. History length and stride determine the look-back window without reducing the depth update rate. During training, a one-frame delay emulates sensor latency. 3.3 Terrain Edge Contact Penalization Driven by energy efficiency, RL agents naturally tend to minimize effort by taking shorter steps and often placing their feet on the nearest terrain edges [ 4 ] . While this strategy can yield high success rates in simulation due to simplified contact physics, it significantly increases the sim-to-real gap. In the real world, contact dynamics at structural edges are far more unpredictable than in simulation, and stepping partially on them frequently leads to catastrophic slippage and deployment failures. To mitigate this, we propose a soft-constraint mechanism that penalizes foot contacts near sharp geometric boundaries. First, we use a detector to find the sharp edges of the terrain mesh. Second, we attach a set of “volume points” to the robot’s feet. By penalizing these points when they penetrate edge regions, the policy learns to select more stable, centered foot positions rather than risky ones. 3.3.1 Terrain Edge Detector First, we identify sharp terrain edges by comparing the dihedral angle between adjacent faces to a predefined threshold τ . To enhance computational efficiency, the resulting raw edges are further processed to filter noise and concatenate short segments. The edge processing step employs a greedy concatenation strategy to merge fragmented segments and reduce the total number of primitives. More details can be found in subsection A.10 . We leverage NVIDIA Warp [ 27 ] to perform massively parallel distance queries, computing the penetration depth of each point relative to the previously constructed spatial collision grid 𝒮 . To discourage unstable foot-ground interactions near terrain edges, we define a penalty term that considers both the geometric penetration and the foot velocity, following [ 53 ] . For each point i ∈ 𝒫 , let 𝐝 i be its penetration offset and 𝐯 i be its linear velocity in the world frame. The penalty term r v ​ o ​ l is formulated as: r v ​ o ​ l = ∑ i = 1 | 𝒫 | ‖ 𝐝 i ‖ ⋅ ( ‖ 𝐯 i ‖ + ϵ ) , (1) where ϵ = 10 − 3 is a small constant for numerical stability. This formulation ensures that the policy is penalized more heavily for high-velocity impacts or scraping motions near terrain edges, thereby encouraging the robot to seek stable footholds. 3.4 Position-based Velocity Command Generation During training, uniformly sampled velocity commands may cause “reward hacking” where the robot spins in place rather than traversing obstacles [ 4 ] . Prior fixes via reward shaping [ 5 ] or goal-based commands [ 50 , 52 ] either lack speed control or produce infeasible targets. We instead generate targets from reachable flat terrain patches and derive speed-controlled commands toward them. We identify reachable flat regions on the terrain mesh via ray-casting: a candidate location is accepted as a navigation target only if the height variation within radius r stays below threshold δ , ensuring targets lie on stable ground rather than slopes or unreachable areas (see algorithm 1 and Figure 9 ). After generating the flat patches, we periodically select one as the navigation goal. The robot’s velocity commands are then generated based on the relative position of this goal. Let 𝐩 g ​ o ​ a ​ l B = [ x g , y g ] T represent the goal position in the robot’s base frame. The desired linear velocity v x and angular velocity ω z are calculated as follows: v x = clip ​ ( k v ⋅ x g , 0 , v m ​ a ​ x ) , (2) ω z = clip ​ ( k ω ⋅ atan2 ​ ( y g , x g ) , − ω m ​ a ​ x , ω m ​ a ​ x ) , (3) where k v and k ω are the linear and angular stiffness coefficients. Because we use the forward camera on the robot’s head link, we only focus on forward locomotion and heading alignment; therefore, the lateral velocity command v y is set to zero. The velocity limits ( v m ​ a ​ x , ω m ​ a ​ x ) are adaptively adjusted based on the terrain category. However, relying solely on these position-based commands can limit the robot’s ability to learn in-place turning, as the goal is usually far away. To address this, we assign pure turning commands to a small subset of agents on flat terrain. For these agents, we set v x = 0 and provide a random ω z , forcing the policy to learn effective in-place rotation maneuvers. This combination improves the overall maneuverability of the robot across different environments. 3.5 Policy Training with Adversarial Motion Priors We adopt the AMP framework [ 33 ] to shape natural locomotion style. Walking and running policies use distinct reference datasets drawn from MPC-generated motion, retargeted human mocap, and running clips from LAFAN [ 15 ] . We train the gaits separately because joint training produces fast walking with long strides rather than natural running. The discriminator operates on short state sequences rather than single transitions to better capture motion dynamics. Full dataset composition, discriminator formulation, and style-reward details are provided in subsection A.9 . For hardware deployment, we use specialized policies for stair walking, platform walking, and running, while retaining the general walking policy for the remaining walking tasks ( subsection A.8 ). To switch policies, the robot first stops, transitions to a standing policy, and then switches to the selected locomotion policy. As illustrated in Figure 5 , the robot successfully performs various locomotion primitives across challenging terrains. Notably, the robot achieves a maximum running speed of 2.5 ​ m/s and successfully traverses difficult obstacles, including high platforms up to 32 ​ cm and discrete gaps with a width of 50 ​ cm . During these deployments, the policy uses depth observations acquired at 60 Hz to react to terrain changes. To quantify the system’s reliability, we conduct 10 trials for each evaluated terrain–gait combination. A trial is successful if the robot reaches the endpoint and stops stably; falling before completion is counted as a failure. Figure 6 reports the success rates for these combinations; absent bars indicate combinations that were not evaluated. Furthermore, we conduct a long-duration test to evaluate the stability of the system over a prolonged period. The robot is tasked with continuous locomotion across multiple staircases and flat surfaces. It successfully maintains its balance and walks for 4 minutes without falls or physical assistance. This sustained performance highlights the effectiveness of our control framework and its potential for deployment in complex, long-range real-world tasks. 4.2 Edge-aware Penalization Mechanism As introduced in subsection 3.3 , we utilize the Terrain Edge Detector and Volumetric Point Penetration Penalization to improve foothold safety. As illustrated in Figure 8 , with edge-aware penalization enabled, the robot tends to seek larger safety margins, effectively placing its feet away from terrain edges to maintain stable contact. To quantitatively evaluate this effect, we measure the Success Rate and Mean Foot Landing Area Fraction in simulation across various challenging terrains. The landing-area metric quantifies the fraction of the foot area supported by the terrain surface, reported on a scale from 0 to 1. To eliminate flat-ground dilution, it is computed exclusively when the robot’s base is within the interaction range of the obstacle. We test each policy for 10,000 timesteps with 1,000 robots. reimplemented external baseline and several ablated variants: • Single-frame Depth (w/o depth history) : The policy receives only the current depth image instead of the strided temporal history. • Uniform Command (w/o position-based) : The policy is trained using standard uniform velocity sampling with heading commands. • No Motion Prior (w/o AMP) : The Adversarial Motion Prior is removed, training the policy solely through task-related rewards. • Blind Policy (w/o depth) : The policy relies exclusively on proprioceptive feedback. • Height Map (10 Hz) : We use a practical elevation-map update rate of 10 Hz [ 44 ] , compared with 60 Hz raw depth acquisition in our system. • HPL [ 54 ] : We reimplement the humanoid parkour learning baseline in our training environment for comparison, as the original code is not open-sourced and the original work targets the Unitree H1 robot trained in IsaacGym. We evaluate each policy with 1,000 robots in simulation and record their success rates and the mean reaching time. As shown in Table 2 , our method achieves the highest success rates among both ablated variants and HPL, confirming the effectiveness of the complete framework on complex terrains. Our depth-based configuration outperforms the 10 Hz height-map baseline at their respective practical operating rates. AMP is an existing training component adopted in our framework. Removing it ( w/o AMP ) reduces success rates to 0% on Small Box, Stair Up, and Platform Up, indicating its importance for terrains requiring large leg lifts in our setting. The w/o position-based variant degrades severely on Large Box (23.1%) and Platform Up (11.7%), confirming that position-based command generation is essential for directing the robot toward challenging obstacles. The blind policy ( w/o depth ) fails on small and elevated structures where foothold precision requires visual feedback. 5 Conclusion and Limitations In this paper, we present an extensible end-to-end perceptive locomotion framework for humanoid robots. By integrating a novel volumetric edge-aware penalization mechanism with position-based velocity command generation, our approach achieves highly dynamic behaviors on complex terrains. Extensive experiments in simulation and real-world deployment demonstrate that our framework enables humanoid robots to perform agile running and stable walking across a variety of challenging environments. Limitations. A single forward-facing depth camera limits backward and lateral awareness. Hardware deployment uses specialized policies for stair walking, platform walking, and running; learning a unified policy remains challenging because joint training can cause mode collapse. Evaluation is limited to the Unitree G1 and static environments, with only 10 trials per evaluated terrain–gait combination. These results do not establish generalization across robot platforms or performance in dynamic environments. Failure-recovery capability is also limited. Future work includes broader perceptual coverage, unified policies, recovery behaviors, and more extensive hardware evaluation. These criteria prevent the policy from exploring physically invalid states or dangerous configurations. The specific thresholds and parameters used in our MDP are detailed below: • Time Out: The episode terminates naturally if the maximum episode duration is reached. • Terrain Out-of-Bounds: The episode ends if the robot moves beyond the valid terrain boundaries. A distance buffer of 2.0 m is applied to allow for minor deviations at the boundaries. • Illegal Contact: To penalize falls, the episode terminates if the robot’s torso ( torso_link ) experiences a contact force exceeding a threshold of 1.0 N. • Bad Orientation: To maintain stability, the episode ends if the tilt angle of the robot’s base exceeds 1.0 rad relative to the gravity vector. • Insufficient Root Height: The episode terminates if the robot’s root height drops below 0.5 m relative to the environment origin, indicating a fall or a failure to stand up. To improve hardware stability, we fine-tune three specialized policies from this checkpoint: stair walking , platform walking , and running . Each specialized policy is trained for an additional 40,000 iterations. During hardware deployment, the specialized policies handle their respective tasks, while the general walking policy handles the remaining walking tasks. Post-training is conducted on flat ground combined with the relevant target terrain. To focus each policy on its intended operating regime, we narrow the velocity command range compared to general training: the walking policies use v x ∈ [ 0.45 , 0.55 ] m/s and the running policy uses v x ∈ [ 1.95 , 3.05 ] m/s. The lateral command is fixed at v y = 0 for all post-training runs. All other training hyperparameters remain identical to those used in the general policy. A.9 AMP Training Details A.9.1 Reference Dataset Composition The reference dataset 𝒟 is collected at 50 Hz from three sources: 1. Synthetic Data : Walking motions from an MPC controller [ 10 ] . 2. Human Motion : Mocap data captured via the NOKOV system, including stair and platform traversal, retargeted to the robot via GMR [ 2 ] . 3. Running Motion : High-speed running clips from the LAFAN dataset [ 15 ] . To avoid mode collapse, walking and running policies use separate datasets: 𝒟 walk combines sources 1 and 2 ( T = 379.62 s); 𝒟 run uses source 3 ( T = 1.54 s, one gait cycle). Symmetric augmentation is applied to all datasets. Joint training produces fast walking with long strides rather than natural running. We attribute this to the lack of transition data between walking demonstrations (up to 1 m/s) and running demonstrations (above 2 m/s), together with the smaller amount of running data. The running policy is therefore initialized from the general walking checkpoint and trained separately using 𝒟 run , as described in subsection A.8 . Despite the narrow running distribution, the policy naturally generalizes to turning and climbing at inference time. This suggests that AMP requires as little as a single complete gait cycle to effectively imitate a target motion style, making it practical to deploy with minimal reference data. A.9.2 Discriminator and Style Reward The discriminator D ⁡ ( 𝐒 ) receives short temporal sequences rather than single state pairs, enabling it to capture motion dynamics. It is trained with a least-squares (MSE) loss: L D = 𝔼 ℳ ​ [ ( D ⁡ ( 𝐒 ) − 1 ) 2 ] + 𝔼 𝒫 ​ [ ( D ⁡ ( 𝐒 ) + 1 ) 2 ] , (7) where ℳ is the reference dataset and 𝒫 is the policy rollout. Gradient penalty and weight decay are applied for training stability. The style reward fed back to the policy is: r t = max ⁡ [ 0 , 1 − 0.25 ​ ( D ⁡ ( 𝐒 t ) − 1 ) 2 ] . (8) Compared to binary cross-entropy and log-based rewards, this combination of an MSE loss and a quadratic reward yields smoother, non-saturating gradients, improving convergence toward the reference motion manifold. We use the 29-DoF Unitree G1 humanoid robot for both simulation and physical deployment. The proprioceptive history length is h = 8 steps, and the depth history uses m = 8 strided frames with stride ℓ . Hardware and Perception: For real-world deployment, we use the factory-integrated Intel RealSense D435i depth camera (standard on Unitree G1, no hardware modification). Depth images are captured at 60 Hz at 480 × 270 , downsampled to 64 × 36 , and cropped to 32 × 18 as policy input. Onboard Deployment: The policy runs at 50 Hz on the onboard NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX via onnxruntime . Depth acquisition and preprocessing run asynchronously at 60 Hz to minimize latency. Operator Guidance: During hardware deployment, a human operator provides velocity commands through a remote controller, while the policy generates joint position targets from onboard observations. The position-based command generation procedure is used during training. Hardware operation therefore consists of perceptive locomotion under operator guidance. Policy changes follow a stop–stand–switch procedure: the robot stops, transitions to a standing policy, and then switches to the selected locomotion policy. Safety and Broader Impact: Perceptive locomotion can extend humanoid operation to uneven outdoor terrain, but falls and unintended motion can endanger nearby people. Hardware testing should therefore remain under continuous human supervision, with an accessible emergency-stop mechanism. Tests in public spaces should use a clearly marked exclusion zone that keeps bystanders outside the robot’s operating and potential fall area. Given the limited failure-recovery capability, the reported results do not establish safety for unsupervised operation around people.
